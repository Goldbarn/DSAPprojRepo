FERNANDO NORONHA, Feb. 20th.--Judging from my observations during our few hours here, the island’s constitution is volcanic, though probably of no recent date. Its most remarkable feature is a conical hill about one thousand feet high, with an exceedingly steep upper part that overhangs its base on one side. Its phonolite rock divides into irregular columns. At first sight these isolated masses suggest a sudden upheaval in a semi-fluid state. At St. Helena, however, I ascertained that pinnacles of nearly similar form and constitution owed their formation to melted rock injected into yielding strata, which thus supplied moulds for these gigantic obelisks. Wood covers the whole island, but the dry climate gives it no appearance of luxuriance. Half-way up the mountain, great masses of columnar rock gave a pleasing effect to the nearer scenery. Laurel-like trees shaded them; other trees, covered with fine pink flowers but without a single leaf, ornamented them.
