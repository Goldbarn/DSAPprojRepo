Judging by its frequent repetition, one objection appears chiefly relied upon: that the House of Representatives, being insufficiently numerous to receive every class of citizens, cannot combine every portion of the community's interests and feelings, nor secure due sympathy between representatives and constituents. This argument wears a specious, seducing form, well calculated to seize the prejudices of its audience. Yet attentive dissection reveals merely fair-sounding words. Its apparent object is, first, impracticable; in the sense contended for, it is also unnecessary. I reserve discussion of the representative body's sufficiency in numbers for another place. Here I shall content myself with examining the particular use made of the contrary supposition in reference to our immediate subject of inquiry.
